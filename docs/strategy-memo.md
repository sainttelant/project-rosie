# The Default Treatment

*Strategy memo · How Project Rosie becomes the standard of care for canine cancer in North America — written as a backcast from 2031*

**For:** prospective co-founders and advisors · **Stage:** pre-seed, MVP built · **HQ:** Toronto, GTA

---

## Thesis

The science is no longer the question. Moderna and Merck's Phase 3 melanoma readout (August 2026) proved personalized neoantigen mRNA vaccines work in humans; Gamgee's $4M seed and YC batch proved venture capital will fund the canine version. Neither has built the thing that decides who wins.

That thing is manufacturing logistics. **This is a manufacturing company with a bioinformatics moat — not a bioinformatics company with a manufacturing problem.**

---

## Backcast — what had to go right

Working backwards from the outcome, seven things carried the story. Note how few of them are science.

### 1. Cost per dose broke below the price of chemotherapy

Sequencing and neoantigen calling were already near-zero marginal cost in 2026. Everything hinged on driving a personalized batch from roughly $150K to under $5K landed. Three compounding moves did it: one standardized construct architecture so every dog's vaccine was the same validated process with a different sequence; a shift from contract manufacturing to owned benchtop-scale production once volume justified it; and release testing batched across parallel patient runs instead of paid per dog.

### 2. The autogenous-vaccine pathway was ridden early and hard

Competitors tried to license a product and stalled. A per-patient vaccine legally resembles an autogenous biologic — a category CFIA and USDA already know how to regulate — so the quality system qualified the *platform and facility*, not each vaccine. A pre-submission meeting with CCVB in year one shaped the facility design before a single dog was dosed.

### 3. Evidence came from one indication, done properly

Specialists prescribe on published, controlled data in a specific cancer — not on compassionate-use anecdotes. One high-incidence, poor-prognosis indication (osteosarcoma, hemangiosarcoma, or oral melanoma) run as a controlled trial at OVC Guelph through the NIH Comparative Oncology Trials Consortium made the data multi-site and US-credible from the start. One clean survival-benefit publication outperformed twenty case reports.

### 4. Distribution rode the specialty referral network

A quarter of dogs get cancer, but treatment decisions funnel through a few hundred board-certified oncologists in North America. The company scaled the way IDEXX and Antech scaled diagnostics: the vet ships a sample, gets back a treatment, bills the client. It never touched the dog. When the top fifty specialty hospitals held standing accounts, "default" followed mechanically.

### 5. Insurance made price a non-event

At $8–15K the vaccine priced inside the existing chemotherapy and radiation envelope. Because pet insurers already reimburse cancer treatment at 70–90% after deductible, no new reimbursement category had to be invented — it only had to be coded as cancer treatment. Getting explicitly onto major carriers' covered-treatment lists converted the vaccine from a luxury purchase into a checkbox.

### 6. The team made it credible before the data did

What opened OVC, CCRM, and the seed round in year one was a board-certified veterinary oncologist co-founder and a real scientific advisory board. Investors were not underwriting the science — Moderna had already done that. They were underwriting whether this specific team could run a regulated clinical operation.

### 7. The human-platform thesis stayed upside, never the operating plan

The same infrastructure eventually compresses cost for human personalized vaccines, and that belongs in the Series B deck. Companies that blurred the line drowned in human-regulatory gravity before their veterinary business matured. The dog market alone carried the valuation.

## Pre-mortem — the three ways it dies

Same backcast, inverted. Each of these is guardable today, and expensive to fix later.

| Risk | Failure mode |
|---|---|
| **Cost** | Manufacturing never breaks below ~$30K per dose and the product stays a luxury niche — a few hundred wealthy owners a year, no path to standard of care, no venture-scale outcome. |
| **Evidence** | Trial one is underpowered or sits in a weak indication, the survival signal comes back ambiguous, and specialist adoption is poisoned for years. Oncologists do not revisit a treatment that failed to separate from control. |
| **Speed** | Year one is spent polishing the pipeline instead of closing the oncologist co-founder and the manufacturing relationship, and a US competitor with Gamgee's momentum locks up the consortium trial sites first. |

---

## Year-one priority order

Strictly ordered. Each step is the unlock for the one after it, and the manufacturing conversation starts before it is needed because it has the longest lead time.

1. **Close the veterinary oncology co-founder** — a DVM with ACVIM oncology board certification and trial experience. Everything downstream (trial site, regulator, investor) evaluates the team before the technology.
2. **Open the CFIA pre-submission conversation** — establish the autogenous / research-use pathway with CCVB before facility and protocol decisions harden.
3. **Secure the manufacturing relationship** — CCRM's Centre for Cell and Vector Production at MaRS is the closest Canadian analogue to the subsidized academic route that produced Rosie's vaccine at UNSW. Longest lead time of anything on this list.
4. **Select the indication for trial one** — high incidence, poor prognosis, dissatisfying standard of care, motivated owners. This choice determines whether the resulting publication moves specialists.
5. **Raise the seed on that package** — co-founder, regulatory pathway, manufacturing partner, and a named indication with a trial site. Not a deck — a critical path with signatures on it.

## Market — what owners already pay

This is not a market that has to be created. Owners already spend at this level on treatments with materially worse odds than what the human melanoma data just demonstrated.

| Current standard of care | Typical cost | Reimbursement |
|---|---|---|
| Surgical tumor removal | $500 – 5,000 | Covered as illness |
| Chemotherapy, full protocol | $3,000 – 10,000 | 70–90% after deductible |
| Radiation therapy | $4,000 – 10,000+ | 70–90% after deductible |
| Combination therapy | $10,000 – 20,000+ | 70–90% after deductible |

Roughly a quarter of dogs develop cancer in their lifetime, with risk rising sharply after age ten. A vaccine priced at $8–15K sits inside the existing envelope rather than above it — which is why the strategic problem is manufacturing cost, not willingness to pay.

## The ask

A working end-to-end pipeline already exists: variant file in, ranked canine neoantigens and a synthesis-ready mRNA construct out, validated on real canine coordinates and deployed publicly. That is the part most people in this space are still pitching on a slide.

What it needs now is a board-certified veterinary oncologist who wants to run the trial that makes this the default, and a manufacturing lead who believes the per-dose cost curve can be broken. If that is you, the next conversation is about the first indication and the first hundred dogs.

## Caveats on the numbers

- **Cost figures are estimates**, assembled from public human-vaccine data — not quotes from a CDMO. The ~$150K starting point is extrapolated from reported human personalized-vaccine manufacturing costs (>$100K/patient); the sub-$5K target is a reasoned target, not a validated quote for a personalized veterinary batch.
- **CCRM's publicly confirmed GMP capability is cell and gene therapy / viral vectors**, not mRNA-LNP specifically. It is the right *kind* of institution and the closest Canadian analogue to UNSW's RNA Institute, but confirm the mRNA-LNP question directly before treating it as settled.
- **Regulatory pathway** reflects CFIA and USDA guidance current as of August 2026 and should be confirmed in a pre-submission meeting.
- Cost and reimbursement ranges are North American market estimates and vary by region, insurer, and indication.
